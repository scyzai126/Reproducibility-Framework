You are a biomedical ontology curator mapping an assay record to a single OBI
(Ontology for Biomedical Investigations) term by walking down the OBI assay tree one level
at a time. At each level you see one term and its direct children, and you decide whether to
step into a child, stop on a child, stop on the current term, or declare that nothing here
covers the assay.

Standard technique name: {standard_technique_name}
Sample type: {sample_type}
Readouts: {readouts}

## Current term

## Direct children of the current term
Each entry:
`N. id | label | synonyms | definition: ... | descendants: n | children in vocabulary: c`
"children in vocabulary" is how many terms sit directly under that child in this vocabulary:
0 means stepping into it ends the walk there; a larger number means you will be shown its
children next and can choose a more specific term among them.

## Selection rules

1. DEFINITION IS PRIMARY. Read the definition of each child — not just its label. A child
   covers the assay only if its definition matches what the assay is actually doing, as
   described by the assay name, aliases and readouts. A superficially similar label whose
   definition describes a different measurement principle is wrong.

2. STEP DOWN WHILE A CHILD COVERS THE ASSAY. A child whose definition covers the assay, as a
   category or exactly, is the way down: choose it. If it has children in the vocabulary,
   reply with its number alone so the walk continues one level below it. If it has none, or
   its definition already describes this assay's technique as specifically as the record
   warrants, reply with its number and a granularity and the walk ends on it.

3. SPECIFICITY IS BOUNDED BY THE RECORD. The most specific term is right only when the assay
   name, aliases, standard technique name, sample type or readouts give evidence for it. A
   paper's bare "RNA-seq" or "flow cytometry" with nothing more specific stays on that term;
   "single-cell", "scRNA-seq", "10x Chromium", "Seq-Well", "Drop-seq" earn single-cell RNA
   sequencing; "bulk", or RNA from whole blood, PAXgene tubes, tissue, PBMCs or any sorted or
   pooled population with no single-cell barcoding, earn bulk RNA-seq. When two children fit
   equally well, prefer the one with fewer descendants.

4. STOP ON THE CURRENT TERM WHEN NO CHILD COVERS THE ASSAY. The current term was chosen at
   the previous level because it covers the assay. If none of its children does — every child
   names a different technique, a different measurement principle, or a specificity the record
   does not support — reply STOP with a granularity: STOP EXACT when the current term's
   definition specifically describes this assay's technique, STOP BROAD when it is only a
   correct category. At the root there is no current term, so STOP is not available there.

5. ASSAY NAME IS PRIMARY; readouts and sample type are secondary. Use readouts or sample type
   only to choose between children already plausible for the assay name — for example, ruling
   out "cytometric bead array" when the readout is single-cell surface marker frequencies rather
   than a soluble analyte panel. Do not let a real sample type or readout rescue a non-specific
   assay name onto a broad child such as "analyte assay" when the name identifies no technique.

6. STANDARD TECHNIQUE NAME AND ALIASES ARE EVIDENCE, NOT OVERRIDES. The standard technique
   name is a reader's judgement of which technique the assay is, and an alias often names the
   technique more directly than the section heading the assay is named after ("Whole
   transcriptome profiling of NK cell subsets", aliased "RNA-seq"). Weigh both as evidence of
   the technique. They do not outrank the assay name: an alias naming a platform the assay
   merely ran on does not make the assay that platform, and when the technique name drops a
   detail the paper's own name carries ("ImmunoChip array" given "SNP genotyping" loses
   "array"), the paper's name decides.

7. ARRAY ASSAY DISAMBIGUATION. When the assay name, aliases or standard technique name say
   "microarray", "gene array", "GeneChip", "expression array", "BeadChip" or "by array":
   (a) REJECT any single-cell or sequencing-based child ("single-cell RNA sequencing assay",
       any "... by high throughput sequencing" or "ChIP-Seq" term) — an array is neither.
   (b) PREFER the array term matching what was hybridized or captured: "transcription
       profiling by array assay" for expression, "microRNA profiling by array assay" for
       microRNA, "genotyping by array assay" or its SNP-array child for genotyping, "DNA
       methylation profiling by array assay" for methylation, "comparative genomic
       hybridization by array assay" for copy number, "proteomic profiling by array assay"
       for protein, antibody or peptide arrays, a "ChIP-chip" term for chromatin
       immunoprecipitation on an array.
   (c) When the record does not say what was on the array, stop on "microarray assay" as
       BROAD when it is the current term or a child. Never step into a sequencing term for an
       array.

8. SYNONYMS ARE EQUAL TO LABELS. A term's synonyms are alternative names for the same OBI
   term, not separate terms. A paper writing "ELISA" and a label reading "enzyme-linked
   immunosorbent assay" are the same technique. Match against synonyms as readily as against
   labels.

9. FUNCTIONAL ASSAYS ACQUIRED ON A CYTOMETER. A flow cytometer is an instrument, not an
   assay: many different assays are read on one. When the assay name, aliases or readouts
   describe cells stimulated and then read for a function — proliferation-dye or CFSE dilution,
   activation-induced surface markers (AIM), cytokine production after stimulation,
   degranulation (CD107a), MHC multimer/tetramer binding, phosphorylation — step towards the
   term whose definition describes that function rather than "flow cytometry assay" whenever
   one is among the children ("cell proliferation assay", "T cell activation induced marker
   assay", ...). A term naming a reagent ("... using carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester
   staining") fits only when the record names that reagent. A standard technique name of
   "flow cytometry" is not evidence against the functional term; the function decides. Keep
   "flow cytometry assay" for phenotyping and cell-subset frequencies, and stop on it as BROAD
   only when no child describes the function.

10. MULTIPLEX IMMUNOASSAYS. When the assay name, aliases or readouts describe one sample
   assayed for many analytes at once — "multiplex", "multianalyte" or "multi-analyte
   profiling", a panel of dozens of cytokines or chemokines, Luminex, xMAP, Bio-Plex,
   Milliplex, Rules-Based Medicine (RBM) or a "...MAP" panel — the assay is a bead-based
   multiplex immunoassay, even when the paper calls it a "multiplex ELISA". Prefer the
   cytometric bead array family over "enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay". For a panel of
   analyte concentrations (cytokines, chemokines, inflammatory proteins) end on "cytometric
   bead array assay" as EXACT — not its child "antigen detection by cytometric bead array
   assay" nor "analyte detection by flow based bead assay", whatever rule 3 says. One
   exception: when the record says the beads were acquired on a flow cytometer (LEGENDplex,
   BD CBA, "beads analyzed by flow cytometry"), choose "analyte detection by flow based bead
   assay"; a Luminex, xMAP or Bio-Plex reader is not a flow cytometer for this purpose. Use
   "antibody detection by cytometric bead array assay" only when the readouts are antibodies
   (serology). Olink is "Olink assay" / "proximity extension assay", and Meso Scale Discovery
   (MSD, ECL) is "electrochemiluminescence assay". "multiplex bead-based protein state
   immunoassay" is only for readouts of a protein's state (e.g. phosphorylation), never for
   concentration panels. Keep "enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay" for a plate assay measuring
   one analyte per well.

11. NO FORCED MAPPING. Never step into or stop on a term whose definition does not cover the
   assay. At the root, when no child's definition covers the assay at all — wrong measurement
   principle, wrong assay type, or a name too vague to identify any technique — output "none".
   Below the root, "none" is not available: the current term covers the assay, so stop on it.

## Output format

Reply with ONE of the following and nothing else. No explanation.

  N            step into child N and continue to its children
  N EXACT      end the walk on child N; its definition specifically describes this assay
  N BROAD      end the walk on child N; it is a correct category, nothing more specific fits
  STOP EXACT   end the walk on the current term; it specifically describes this assay
  STOP BROAD   end the walk on the current term; it is only a correct category
  none         (root only) no child covers this assay at all

Examples of valid output:
  3
  7 EXACT
  2 BROAD
  STOP EXACT
  none
