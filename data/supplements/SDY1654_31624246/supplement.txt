[S128] Reviewers' comments: Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): revised ms is acceptable Reviewer #4 (Remarks to the Author): Szabo and colleagues generated a scRNA-seq dataset comprising more than 50,000 resting and activated T cells from lung, lymph nodes, and bone marrow samples collected from two deceased donors, and from blood samples collected from two healthy donors. [S129] Through bioinformatics analysis, they identified: • A tissue-specific gene signature that distinguishes tissue-resident T cells from circulating T cells; • T-cell transcriptional fingerprints shared by blood- and tissue-specific T cells; • Two novel candidate markers of CD4+ T cell activation that they could validate in vitro. [S130] They further showed how to leverage the data generated in this study (referred to as “reference map”) to investigate the cellular composition of external scRNA-seq datasets. [S131] The amount of experimental and bioinformatic work is impressive and sheds some light on an important and debated topic in immunology: T-cell phenotypes and transcriptional programs in different tissue contexts. [S132] However, I have a few major concerns regarding: • The possibility to consider this dataset as a definitive T-cell reference map in blood and tissues; • The robustness of the bioinformatics methods and results given the state of the art in scRNA-seq data analysis; • The clarity of the described analytical approaches. [S133] In the following, I first motivate and detail these concerns, and then provide some suggestions on other major and minor points. [S134] REFERENCE MAP The authors stress in several sections of the manuscript how this dataset can be considered a reference map for future studies. [S135] However, due to the limitations in terms of sample size (two donors per tissues) and total tissues (four), these data might under-represent the real biological variability of T-cell transcriptional programs in different individuals and tissues. [S136] Thus, I would suggest toning down this kind of claims (e.g. [S137] “comprehensive baseline of healthy T cells states in humans”, l. [S138] 76-77) and discuss openly possible limitations of the present study in the Discussion section. [S139] Despite these limitations, this dataset represents a valuable resource that should be rendered more easily usable, especially if the intent is to propose it as a reference for future single-cell studies. [S140] For instance, how can this “reference map” be reconstructed easily from the data available on GEO and used for interpretation of the external dataset? [S141] This kind analysis could be made easily accessible by providing the preprocessed reference in a convenient format, together with some (R?) code to run the comparison (e.g. [S142] to generate plots similar to those of Fig. [S143] 6b). [S144] Alternatively, tools based on reference count matrices, like scmap (PMID: 29608555), could be used. [S145] I would include some information in this sense to guide the readers in this kind of analysis. [S146] METHOD ROBUSTNESS The authors present the bioinformatics methods adopted as “innovative” (l. [S147] 87), which I agree with. [S148] However, the field of scRNA-seq data analysis is fast-paced and far from being standardized. [S149] Many methods are available for the same analytical task, rely heavily on parameter setting and data preprocessing, and often return different results. [S150] Thus, the robustness of methods and results has to be endorsed by using orthogonal methods. [S151] For instance, I would suggest considering these methods in parallel with the analyses already performed: • Projection of a query scRNA-seq dataset on a reference dataset with scmap (PMID: 29608555); • Integration of different datasets with Seurat 3 (PMID: 31178118, see the description about the identification of “anchor” cells); • Single-cell classification with SingleR (PMID: 30643263). [S152] These methods are also useful to confirm the projections of Fig. [S153] 2 and 6. [S154] Finally, approaches like those implemented Seurat might be useful to integrate and harmonize several T-cell datasets to really provide an ultimate reference map. [S155] This is probably out of the scope of the current manuscript, but still worth mentioning in the Discussion section as a future development. [S156] CLARITY OF METHOD DESCRIPTION In the Methods section, the description of the cell-type identification and scHPF analyses should be improved to facilitate understanding and reproducibility. [S157] I understand that cell-type identification was performed in three major steps: 1. [S158] Clustering for each donor separately, all tissues together, expecting K=12 clusters. [S159] 2. [S160] Cell-type classification. [S161] 3. [S162] Further selection of cell-type specific genes to be shown in Fig. [S163] 1c. [S164] The Methods section should describe clearly how steps 2 and 3 where performed, so to allow reproducibility. [S165] Regarding step 2, I point out that recent best practices (PMID: 31217225) suggest integrating automatic (like SingleR) and manual approaches (based on prior knowledge of marker genes) to annotate cell types in large datasets like this one. [S166] Similarly, for scHPF analysis: 1. [S167] Identification of factors specific for each sample (i.e. [S168] tissue-donor) using scHPF. [S169] 2. [S170] Clustering of the factor-factor correlation matrix to identify major modules. [S171] 3. [S172] Annotation/interpretation of modules. [S173] Regarding step 2, I think the terminology is a bit confusing: are “modules” and “models” the same thing? [S174] Also, the sentence about the seven models mentioned at l. [S175] 629-630 is probably too premature and confusing because, at this point, only factors are identified, not modules. [S176] Step 3 requires a careful description of how the modules were annotated and why two modules could not be annotated. [S177] If possible, module-specific genes should be reported also for these two modules in Suppl. [S178] Table 7. [S179] Please, also double check that the gene sets identified and used in Fig. [S180] 1c (differentiating T cell lineages), Fig. [S181] 3a-c (DEGs), and Fig. [S182] 3e (tissue signature) are fully reported in the Supplementary Material and referenced in the main text, and that the exact procedures for their identification is described in a Methods sub-section. [S183] The underlying methods and final gene-set results are not straightforward to picture out from the current manuscript. [S184] MAJOR POINTS I do not agree with the following sentence (l. [S185] 113-115): “T cells from BM and LN co-clustered while LG T cells were more distinct (Fig. [S186] 1b), consistent with T cell subset composition differences in these sites from phenotype analysis”. [S187] UMAP plots show clustering of cells based on similarities of the transcriptional profiles of single-cells, whereas sample composition is an overall quantification of the different cell subtypes. [S188] These two aspects do not have a 1:1 correspondence but rather provide complementary insights. [S189] Also, the cellular composition is not easy to visualize from overlapping UMAP points. [S190] I would suggest rephrasing, as well as integrating both types of information in these plots (e.g. [S191] Fig. [S192] 6b), by showing both the UMAP plot and, next to it, a small, stacked barplot representing the cellular composition. [S193] In addition, it should be mentioned in the text that the quantification of cell subtypes from scRNA-seq data might be biased by single-cell dissociation efficiencies (PMID: 30345255). [S194] Being this work mainly based on dry- rather than wet-lab work, I am wondering whether you can confirm the results of your “kinetic analysis” of CD4+ T cells (p. [S195] 12) also computationally, for instance through analysis of CD4+ T cell “velocity” (PMID: 30089906) and evolution in pseudotime of the expression of the IFIT3 and NME1 genes (PMID: 24658644). [S196] The transcriptional profiles of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cell were found to be consistent across all tissues, whereas the CD4+ T cell phenotypes were more variable. [S197] Could this result be due to a bias in the set of highly variable genes considered, as many of them were CD4-T-cell-specific, and much fewer CD8-T-cell-specific (as shown in Fig. [S198] 1c heatmap)? [S199] Some interesting and original results might be put in a better light by discussing them more deeply. [S200] For instance, is there anything in the literature supporting the association found in this study between localization of T cells and structural changes? [S201] Also, Treg transcriptional fingerprints have been previously reported to be very plastic and variable across tissues, and in your dataset seem to also differ between donors (Fig. [S202] 1b-c, resting in D1, activated in D2). [S203] Some discussion about the Treg phenotypes found in the different tissues of the present study would be extremely interesting for the scientific community. [S204] Besides Tregs, there is a lot of debate about the variability of immune-cell transcriptional profiles in different tissue and disease contexts, and on whether these differences should be taken into account by deconvolution methods for the quantification of cell subtypes from bulk transcriptomic data (PMID: 29541787). [S205] As you identified both tissue-dependent and -independent markers, a discussion of these aspects might provide valuable guidance for the researchers in this field. [S206] I agree that the current study can provide a basis to study T-cell phenotypes in cancer, but I find the analysis proposed too limited and not really conclusive. [S207] Thus, I would suggest investigating a bit more its relevance for cancer immunology. [S208] One idea could be, for example, to test the prognostic and predictive value of the selected CD4-T-cellactivation markers using data from TCGA or from patients treated with immune checkpoint blockers, respectively. [S209] MINOR POINTS: “CD3+T” (and similar ones) should be corrected to “CD3+ T” (with spaces after “+”). [S210] Please provide some rationale for the parameter setting presented in the Methods section (e.g. [S211] Louvain clustering with K=12 or DMAPS kernel bandwidth of 4). [S212] I would put the heatmaps of Fig. [S213] 1c in a separated panel (Fig. [S214] 1d) and use the same ordering of genes for the two heatmaps so to ease comparison. [S215] In the second plot of Fig. [S216] 3f, the palette does not allow distinguishing well the groups. [S217] For the reasons explained above, I would not call the tissue gene signature “universal” (l. [S218] 148). [S219] Fig. [S220] 3a-c and the relative captions are a bit confusing.
