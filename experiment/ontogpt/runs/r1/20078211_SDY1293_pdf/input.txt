Expression of Genes Associated with
Immunoproteasome Processing of Major
Histocompatibility Complex Peptides Is Indicative
of Protection with Adjuvanted RTS,S Malaria Vaccine

Background.
Patterns of expressed genes in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of persons who were
receiving RTS,S/AS01 or RTS,S/AS02 malaria vaccine and were undergoing experimental challenge with mosquito-
borne falciparum malaria were examined to identify markers associated with protection.
Methods.
Thirty-nine vaccine recipients were assessed at study entry; on the day of the third vaccination; at
24 h, 72 h, and 2 weeks after vaccination; and on day 5 after challenge. Of 39 vaccine recipients, 13 were protected
and 26 were not. Eleven vaccine recipients exhibited delayed onset of parasitemia. All infectivity control subjects
developed parasitemia. Prediction analysis of microarrays identiﬁed genes corresponding with protection. Gene
set enrichment analysis identiﬁed sets of genes associated with protection after the third vaccination and before
challenge.
Results.
After the third vaccination and before challenge, differential expression of genes in the immunopro-
teasome pathway distinguished protected and nonprotected persons. At 5 days after challenge, differential expression
of genes associated with programmed cell death distinguished between subjects protected and not protected from
malaria blood-stage infection.
Conclusions.
The up-regulation of genes associated with the efﬁcient processing of major histocompatibility
complex peptides suggests a potential role of the vaccine in conferring major histocompatibility complex class 1–
mediated protection and may represent a useful surrogate marker of vaccine efﬁcacy without the need for challenge.

through collaboration between the Walter Reed Army
Institute of Research and GlaxoSmithKline Biologicals
[2], is the only vaccine shown to prevent malaria con-
sistently in a portion of vaccine recipients, as evaluated
by mosquito challenge [3–7]. Numerous formulations

Gene expression proﬁling has being successfully used to in-
vestigate host genetic responses and has been applied to predict
the responses to treatment and clinical outcome associated with
several diseases, including cancer [13], HIV disease [14], tu-
berculosis [15], and multiple sclerosis [16]. Gene chip–based
prediction strategies have received approval from the US Food
and Drug Administration as prognostic tools for breast cancer
[17–22]. The gene expression proﬁles of PBMCs collected be-
fore and after immunization with a yellow fever attenuated
vaccine have revealed hundreds of genes that are induced and
suppressed temporally in response to immunization and that
predict polyfunctional immune responses [23, 24].
The goals of the present study were to determine whether
there is a gene expression proﬁle that is predictive of an in-
dividual’s response to the adjuvanted RTS,S malaria vaccine
and to identify the time at which that proﬁle can be discerned
before challenge. Using global gene expression proﬁling, we
identiﬁed expression of genes in the immunoproteasome path-
way that impinge on the efﬁciency of MHC class 1 processing
and subsequent antigen presentation, and that are differentially
expressed in persons protected by the vaccine and are discerned
before human challenge with the pathogen.
SUBJECTS, MATERIALS, AND METHODS
Clinical specimens.
PBMCs were obtained after informed
consent was provided by study volunteers enrolled in a phase
2b trial of adjuvanted RTS,S [7]. The RTS,S vaccine used in
this study consists of sequences of the P. falciparum CSP ex-
pressed in hepatitis B surface antigen and formulated with pro-
prietary adjuvant systems AS01 (MPL, QS21, and liposomes)
or AS02 (MPL, QS21, and an oil-in-water emulsion) [25]. Chal-
lenge was performed with a homologous 3D7 strain of P. fal-
ciparum delivered by 5 bites from infected mosquitoes [26]. To
assess the efﬁciency of the challenge, 12 persons without pre-
vious vaccination were also challenged. Infection with malaria
was determined by detection of parasite in malaria thick blood
smears during the 30 days after challenge. Protection was de-
ﬁned as failure to develop P. falciparum asexual-stage parasi-
temia. Partial protection or delay was deﬁned as a signiﬁcant
prolongation in the prepatent period (48 h), compared with
that noted in the infectivity control subjects [27]. PBMCs were
collected by gradient centrifugation in cell preparation tubes
(Becton Dickinson) and cryopreserved. Samples obtained from
vaccine recipients were assessed for gene expression patterns at
study entry (36 samples); on the day of the third vaccination
(44 samples); at 24 h (43 samples), 72 h (43 samples), and 2
weeks (37 samples) thereafter; and at day 5 after challenge (39
samples). For infectivity controls, samples were collected at
entry and at 5 days post challenge.
Preparation of samples for GeneChip analysis.
Preparation
of cellular RNA and subsequent processing for GeneChip analysis
were performed as described elsewhere [28], by use of the HG-
U133 A 2.0 and HG-U133 Plus 2.0 GeneChips (Affymetrix) and
the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies), to
assess the integrity and quantity of RNA.
GeneChip quality control and data normalization.
Gene-
Chips with a scaling factor 150 and an array outlier percentage
15% on dChip software (2005 version) [29] were eliminated
from further analysis. Genes scored as absent in all samples by
use of GeneChip operating software (GCOS) were eliminated
from analysis. Only fully annotated gene targets were assessed.
CEL ﬁles were normalized at the probe level using the Robust
Multi-chip Average (RMA) method [30] built into the Bio-
Conductor package Affy-1.12.2. RMA is a method of normal-
ization of microarray data that leverages gene expression as-
sessments made on Affymetrix gene chips. For each probe set,
the technique generates an estimated value for the probe and
chip effects that result in the overall pattern of probe set values
observed in the data set. RMA is comprised of 3 calculation
applications that address background correction, normalization
of quantiles, and median polish summarization. Data sets were
generated on Affymetrix U133A 2.0 and Plus 2.0 GeneChips
by use of 11-micron technology, with the former being a subset
of the latter and thus facilitating normalization between data
sets. A 2-step approach using RMA was used to normalize data
sets from these 2 platforms. Data sets from the U133A 2.0 and
Plus 2.0 gene chips were ﬁrst normalized individually. Subse-
quently, the common probe sets were identiﬁed and normalized
using the quantiles calculation in the Affy package (Bio-
Conductor) to generate the ﬁnal normalized data set for further
analysis. The complete portfolio of data sets supporting the

Differential expression.
Differentially expressed genes were
identiﬁed using the statistical program Signiﬁcance Analysis of
Microarrays (SAM; version 3.0) [31], and cluster analysis of
microarray data sets was performed using the MultiExperiment
Viewer (version 4.1.01; available at http://www.jcvi.org.cms
.research.software). SAM identiﬁes genes whose expression has
signiﬁcantly changed, by leveraging a set of gene-speciﬁc t tests.
Genes are assigned a score derived from the change in expres-
sion relative to the standard deviation for all measurements
made for that gene. The percentage of genes considered to be
signiﬁcant by chance is deﬁned by the false discovery rate
(FDR). For SAM, we used the cutoff FDR !1%, which is very
stringent. Principal component analysis, which is an eigen vec-
tor–based multivariate tool that reveals the most informative
trends in complex data sets by identifying the predominant
gradients in data sets, was performed using Partek Pro (version
6.0; Partek).

of gene ontology categories [32]. We used DAVID gene on-

DISCUSSION
The goal of this study was to ascertain whether distinct patterns
of gene expression might be associated with outcome in a chal-
lenge model of P. falciparum malaria in persons immunized
with a partially efﬁcacious vaccine. A second goal of the study
was to further reﬁne these patterns to identify speciﬁc biological
processes that might account for the association. A serendip-
itous accomplishment of the study was identiﬁcation of dis-
criminatory patterns as early as 2 weeks after the third vacci-
nation and before challenge with the pathogen.
Global patterns of gene expression, as shown by PCA, are
distinct in vaccinated subjects who had subsequently been chal-
lenged with malaria sporozoites, compared with nonvaccinated
subjects who were challenged. The effect of vaccination is re-
vealed in the robust differential gene expression pattern ob-
served 24 h after vaccination. SAM indicates that genes asso-
ciated with host inﬂammatory response, apoptosis, and the
protein kinase cascade are up-regulated within 24 h of the third
vaccination in all vaccine recipients, regardless of the outcome
after challenge. This remarkably robust level of gene expression
resolves by 72 h after vaccination and returns to the levels of
gene expression observed before the third vaccination. These
responses are likely associated with host response to the vaccine
and its antigenic components to include the CSPs, the hepatitis
B surface antigen, and the adjuvant. Although not indicative
of protection, the magnitude and temporal association of these
responses reveal the power of single gene expression patterns

between these processes and the outcome after challenge.
It is also is important to consider our observations in light
of the fact that genetic polymorphisms of the human leukocyte
antigen system are known to signiﬁcantly affect immune re-
sponses to vaccines. Because of the diversity of human leukocyte
antigen (HLA) types and the limited number of persons in this
phase 2b clinical trial for which partial HLA data have been
generated, insufﬁcient statistical power prevents correlation of
HLA type with the 3 outcome groups in our study. It will be
critical to include assessment of the HLA type of individuals
participating in future, large-scale trials of the RTS,S vaccine.
Our observations are made in the context of the RTS,S vaccine
only, and we cannot discern absolutely whether they are as-
sociated with speciﬁc characteristics of either the RTS,S vaccine
or the individual volunteers who failed to be protected by the
RTS,S vaccine on challenge.

could be inﬂuenced by the introduction of the pathogen. To
address this argument, we undertook assessment of gene ex-
pression patterns 2 weeks after the third vaccination but before
challenge. PAM-R analysis at this time point did not have the

power to resolve subtle changes in gene expression associated

most subtle changes in gene expression that could be speciﬁcally
associated with protection, high-resolution GSEA, which fur-

is observed in GSEA of the vaccine-naive individuals at study

Although CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were not assessed
in our analysis of the total PBMC population, the genes iden-
tiﬁed in the immunoproteasome pathway—PSME2, PSMB9,
PSMB6, and PSMA4—play complex, pivotal roles in the efﬁ-
ciency of MHC peptide processing and subsequent antigen pre-
sentation [35–37]. The prognostic signiﬁcance of immunopro-
teosome gene expression was recently found to be associated
with the degree abnormal histopathologic grade in renal cell
carcinoma [38]. Low-level immunoproteasome subunit ex-
pression was associated with shortened duration of survival in
patients with renal cell carcinoma, indicating that abnormal
antigen processing/presentation leads to escape from immu-
nologic surveillance. In our study, immunoproteasome control
of the selective protein degradation necessary for MHC class I
and class II antigen processing and the key role of IFN-g in
enhancing this process are consistent with the observation that
vaccine recipients with up-regulated expression of immuno-
proteasome pathway genes are protected after challenge.
Identiﬁcation of a set of genes associated with immunopro-
teasome processing of peptides for MHC presentation and pro-
tection by a vaccine is a point of departure for studies of speciﬁc
subpopulations of cells that might conﬁrm and extend our
observations. The identiﬁcation of additional elements of the
immunoproteasome pathway and an evaluation of these genes
and proteins as regulators, second messengers, and potential
predictive biomarkers will contribute to the optimal design and
assessment of vaccines and the further elucidation of the as-
sociation between immunogenicity and protection.
